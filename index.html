🧪 What Is Science Olympiad?

Science Olympiad is a national STEM competition where teams of up to 15 students compete across 23 events spanning biology, chemistry, physics, earth science, engineering, and technology. Think of it as a science track meet: every event runs at the same time, so each person's two or three events count toward one team score. Riverdale competes in Division C (high school) in the NYC North region, which is hosted at Bronx High School of Science.

🏫 How Our Club Works

The club is student-led by our three leaders, Florian Kroker-McCormack, Rohan Varia, and Luke Orlowski, with faculty support from Ms. Monti and Mr. Ahmed. Here is how a season runs:

  • Club periods: Regular meeting times at school where leaders coordinate preparation, answer questions, and track progress.
  • Event assignments: Early in the fall, leaders assign each member to 2–3 events based on interest and team needs. Most events are pairs; Codebusters and Experimental Design take three.
  • Independent study: Much of the preparation happens on your own or with your partner: reading rules, building binders, taking practice tests, and constructing devices for build events.
  • Build sessions: For build events like Hovercraft, Boomilever, or Electric Vehicle, the team books lab or workshop time to construct and test devices. Last year's hovercraft was rebuilt and re-calibrated several times before January.

📋 The Three Types of Events

Every Science Olympiad event falls into one of three categories:

  • 📖 Study Events: Written tests on specific science topics (Astronomy, Disease Detectives, Rocks and Minerals). You usually bring a resource binder you prepared yourself. Success comes from deep knowledge and a well-organized binder.
  • 🔧 Build Events: You design and construct a device before the tournament and test it at competition (Hovercraft, Boomilever, Electric Vehicle, Wright Stuff). These require hands-on engineering, repeated testing, and careful calibration logs.
  • 🧪 Lab / Hybrid Events: Hands-on science tasks performed at the competition, sometimes with a written component (Forensics, Chemistry Lab, Experimental Design). Lab technique and time management decide these.

📅 How to Prepare (A Typical Season)

The season starts in early September and builds toward the regional tournament in late January. Here is what good preparation looks like:

  • Weeks 1–2: Read your event's official rules on soinc.org cover to cover. Open the scioly.org wiki page for your event; it has study guides and strategy notes.
  • Weeks 3–6: Start building your resource binder (study events) or prototyping your device (build events). Work through practice tests from scioly.org/tests.
  • Weeks 7–10: Refine your binder, take timed practice tests, and identify weak areas. Build devices should be fully assembled and tested multiple times before the November 7 invitational.
  • Final 2 weeks: Run full timed practice. Review everything. Confirm logistics and make sure your materials are packed and labeled.

🏆 What Competition Day Looks Like

Tournament day is long and hectic. Knowing the flow helps you stay calm. This is how January 24, 2026 ran at the NYC North Regional:

Early Morning · Arrival
The team meets at school or at the host site, Bronx High School of Science at 75 West 205th Street. Everyone brings their own materials: binders, devices, calculators, safety goggles, and anything else their events require.
Morning · Opening and First Events
After a brief opening, events begin running at the same time in different rooms. You head to your assigned room at your event's scheduled block. Events typically last 30–50 minutes.
Midday · Rotating Events
Events run in blocks throughout the day. Between yours you have downtime: review for the next event or go watch a teammate's device run. Stay near the schedule board for room changes.
Afternoon · Final Events and Awards
The last events wrap up and the tournament ends with an awards ceremony. The top placements in each event are called, then the overall team standings. In 2026 that is where Hovercraft was announced first out of 32 schools.

💡 Tips for New Members

  • Start early. The most common regret from returning members is starting preparation too late. Twenty minutes a day from September adds up to more than forty hours by January.
  • Use scioly.org. The wiki, the test exchange, and the forums are the best resources available for every event on the slate.
  • Communicate with your partner. Most events are pairs. Decide who studies what, split the binder sections, and take practice tests together under time.
  • Ask the leaders. Florian competed in Chemistry Lab, Dynamic Planet, and Experimental Design; Rohan in Codebusters, Designer Genes, Entomology, Helicopter, and Write It Do It; Luke in Boomilever, Disease Detectives, Experimental Design, and Water Quality. Between them they can point you at the right resource for almost any event.
  • Build the binder. For study events, a tabbed binder with diagrams, tables, and worked practice problems is often the difference between a mid-field score and a top-ten finish.
  • Watch a build run. Even if you are in study events, watching Hovercraft or Electric Vehicle calibrate on competition day shows you what a season of testing looks like.
